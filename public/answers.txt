QUESTION 1: PRODUCTION RELIABILITY
------------------------------------------------------------

What's the most pain you've personally experienced running a 24/7 production system? What broke, and what did you change afterward?

ANSWER:

One of the toughest production issues I encountered at Walmart Global Tech involved a failed release of a shared component library used across multiple applications.

The challenging part was the blast radius. A change to a shared dependency could affect several applications and teams simultaneously, making it harder to isolate the root cause. I investigated the failure, identified the problematic library release, and helped roll it back to restore stability.

The experience changed how I approached shared dependencies and production releases. We improved versioning and release coordination across teams to reduce the risk of similar failures.

My biggest takeaway was that reliability isn't just about writing correct code. It's about understanding how changes propagate through a system, limiting their impact, and ensuring there's a safe path to recovery when something goes wrong.


------------------------------------------------------------
QUESTION 2: QUESTIONING BEST PRACTICES
------------------------------------------------------------

What's a "best practice" you disagree with after real-world experience?

ANSWER:

I disagree with the idea that maximizing code reuse is always the right architectural decision.

At Walmart Global Tech, I worked with micro-frontends and shared component libraries designed to reduce duplication and maintain consistency. While that approach had clear benefits, I also experienced its downside when a problematic shared library release introduced regressions across applications.

That experience taught me that excessive reuse can create hidden coupling. When multiple independently developed applications rely on the same shared dependency, even a small change can have a much larger impact than expected.

I now prefer balancing reuse with isolation and independent deployability. I'd rather tolerate a little duplication than introduce dependencies that make releases risky or require multiple teams to coordinate every change.

For me, good engineering isn't about following best practices blindly. It's about understanding the tradeoffs and choosing what makes the system more reliable, maintainable, and easier to evolve.
